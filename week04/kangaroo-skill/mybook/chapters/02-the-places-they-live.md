The kangaroos of the Umbral country do not occupy one habitat. They occupy a series of edges: the upper limit of basalt scree, the boundary between grass and tussock, the narrow band where forest gives way to alpine shrub, and the shifting margin where fresh water meets tide. Each of these places is defined less by a fixed boundary than by a gradient, and each is being reshaped by forces that operate over decades. This chapter describes four of them.

The sources do not use a single name for the animals in these landscapes. The habitat retrospective calls the highland animal *Notamacropus penumbra* [doc10]; the treeline study calls it *Notamacropus umbrica* [doc22]; the lowland fencing study calls its population *Palaeomacropus cinctus lowlandensis* [doc30], where an earlier lowland study used *Macropus lunatus* [doc13]. As in the previous chapter, the names are reported as the sources use them. What matters here is the geography: where the animals are, what the ground offers them, and what is changing.

## The Highland Scree Belt

The basalt screes above 2,000 m in the Umbral Range were once mapped as a stable belt of open rock, frost-shattered debris, and sparse cushion vegetation. A forty-year retrospective combining satellite imagery with field surveys in 1998, 2012, and 2023 shows that the belt is contracting, fragmenting, and moving upslope [doc10]. The headline numbers are stark.

> Scree contracted by 31%, from 1,240 to 856 km², the mean lower boundary rose from 1,985 to 2,115 m, and the number of patches larger than 5 km² fell from 23 to 14.
> — doc10

The contraction is not only a loss of area. It is a loss of connectivity. Large scree patches are fewer, the lower margin is being colonised by mat-forming shrubs, and the remaining high ground is increasingly isolated by scrub-covered slopes and exposed ridgelines [doc10]. For the Shadowtail Kangaroo, the consequences are already measurable. Occupancy of survey squares fell from 64% in 1998 to 47% in 2023, the mean altitude of scat records rose by 135 m, and the estimated adult population declined from about 3,600 to 2,450 [doc10].

The animal's dependence on the substrate explains why the loss bites so directly.

> The species depends on basalt scree for thermal refuge, dust bathing, and access to sparse forage in shaded gullies.
> — doc10

Scree is not merely ground to stand on. It provides cool retreats during hot days, dust for grooming, and shaded pockets where forage persists. When the lower margin is colonised by shrubs, those functions do not simply move uphill intact; they become scarcer and more fragmented. The study is careful about the status of the population: it is not yet at imminent risk of regional extinction, but its range is being compressed into a narrower and more fragmented highland band [doc10].

The proposed East Umbral corridor is the main management response. The route would link the Greywind Plateau population to the reserve through Kestrel Saddle, which still retains 62% of its 1984 scree cover. The authors argue that a corridor designed as a fixed strip would become a warm, open funnel rather than a functioning climate refuge. Their recommendation is a design principle rather than a line on a map.

> We argue that corridor design should protect an elevational gradient rather than a fixed strip.
> — doc10

That principle—protect the gradient, not the boundary—recurs in the other habitats described below.

## Foothill Grasslands and Fire

Below the scree, the foothills are a grass landscape shaped by fire. Historical records describe a regime of frequent, low-intensity surface fires in which small burns rarely exceeded 300 hectares. Between 1985 and 2020 that regime changed: fires became less frequent but much larger, and the grass layer changed with them [doc18].

> Between 1985 and 2020, however, the median fire return interval lengthened from 7.4 to 18.1 years, while the proportion of burned area attributable to fires larger than 5,000 hectares rose from 19% to 63% (Osei & Renn, 2021).
> — doc18

The mechanism is a change in fire size, not simply in the amount of fuel consumed. Small fires maintained a fine-grained mosaic of short, palatable grasses and open inter-tussock spaces. Large fires consume extensive litter, expose mineral soil, and favour resprouting tussocks with deep belowground reserves. In the Umbral foothills, the beneficiaries have been the unpalatable tussocks *Carex argyrophylla* and *Poa spinulosa*, which regenerate quickly after severe burning and then persist as dense, low-quality stands [doc18].

The vegetation data show the shift clearly. Across 42 permanent plots, cover of the palatable short grass *Poa umbrivagans* fell from 34% in 1991 to 17% in 2019, while combined cover of the two tussocks rose from 21% to 46% [doc18]. The change is strongest on mid-slope benches and south-facing convexities, where large fires burned with particular uniformity. In those positions the grass layer remains continuous, but its functional composition has changed from a grazing lawn to a tussock-dominated sward.

For the Crescent Kangaroo, this is a loss of winter forage. The species depends on the short green flush of *P. umbrivagans* and on the winter chenopod *Atriplex hiberna*, patches that remain accessible when tussock stands are tall and fibrous. Dietary studies found that *P. umbrivagans* contributed 46% of winter bite counts in unburned or recently small-burned sites, but only 18% in sites dominated by large-fire regrowth [doc18]. Forage availability across the eastern foothills is estimated to have declined by 31% since 1990, with the steepest losses in years following extensive fire.

The ecological signal is unambiguous, and so is its management implication.

> The ecological signal is clear: fewer small fires and more large ones have favoured unpalatable tussock species and reduced the winter forage base for Crescent Kangaroo.
> — doc18

Reintroducing frequent, patchy small burns, particularly in spring and early autumn, may restore the short-grass mosaic. Small-scale trials in two foothill reserves increased *P. umbrivagans* cover within four years, although tussock seed banks remain a constraint [doc18]. The habitat, in other words, is not simply lost; it is maintained by a disturbance regime that has been interrupted.

## The Treeline Ecotone

Higher and narrower is the treeline ecotone of the Karrin Range, between 1700 and 1900 m. Here two kangaroos overlap for a short season, and their coexistence depends on a fine division of microhabitat and time [doc22].

> Between 1700 and 1900 m in the Karrin Range, Shadowtail and Crescent Kangaroos overlap for a few misty weeks in late summer.
> — doc22

The overlap is not a general mixing. In a study of 42 plots monitored in February and March 2021, Shadowtail kangaroos were strongly associated with shrub thickets and rock outcrops, especially within 15 m of mist-pocket margins, and foraged mainly after dawn. Crescent Kangaroos used open grass swards and ecotone edges, with peak use before sunset. Microhabitat overlap was low (Pianka index 0.31), and forage overlap was lower still (0.24). Temporal separation reduced encounter probability at shared boundaries by an estimated 38% [doc22].

The physical structure of the ecotone matters as much as the food. Mist pockets—shallow saddles and lee slopes where nocturnal condensation persists until mid-morning—are cooler and more humid than the surrounding ground.

> Mist pockets were 3.1 °C cooler and 14 percentage points more humid than adjacent plots in mid-morning.
> — doc22

In plots within 20 m of a mist pocket, Shadowtail pellet counts were 2.3 times higher than in dry outcrops, and Crescent grazing intensity was 1.6 times higher at pocket margins than in interior swards [doc22]. The mist extends the window of palatable forage and reduces heat load for the Shadowtail, while the Crescent benefits from fresh grass growth at the pocket edges without entering dense shrub cover. The result is a habitat that works precisely because it is divided.

> Coexistence depends on a narrow shrub–grass gradient and on mist-pocket hydrology.
> — doc22

The management implication follows directly: protect the hydrology that creates the mist pockets and the shrub–grass boundary that separates the two species. If either is simplified—by drainage, by shrub encroachment, or by uniform grazing—the coexistence mechanism weakens.

## The Saltmarsh Lowlands

The fourth place is the lowest and wettest. The Verrin Saltmarsh Lowlands occupy a shallow embayment where freshwater seepage meets diurnal tides, producing a mosaic of saline mudflats, low sedge meadows, and stranded wrack ridges [doc13]. In winter, troupes of Crescent Kangaroo descend from the dry uplands and use the lowland sedges, mud wallows, and creek margins. In the previous chapter, the lowland population appeared as an ecological agent; here it appears as a population under pressure from fences.

Between 2008 and 2020, a network of pastoral-boundary fences was erected across Verrin Marsh, Lower Kestrel Flats, and Tallow Creek. The fences were intended to separate grazing leases from sensitive wetland, but their alignment followed property lines rather than drainage patterns [doc30]. The result was not a mild inconvenience to movement but a structural break in the population.

> The result has been a hard fragmentation of the lowland crescent kangaroo population into five genetically isolated clusters: North Verrin, Tallow Creek, Brindlemead, Osprey Sedge, and South Kestrel.
> — doc30

Scat genotyping between 2016 and 2022 found pairwise FST values of 0.11–0.19 and effective population sizes of 28–60 adults per cluster [doc30]. Before the fencing programme, seasonal movements of up to 14 km had been recorded between Tallow Creek and North Verrin during wet years. Those movements are now effectively blocked.

> Seasonal movements of up to 14 km recorded before fencing are now blocked.
> — doc30

The habitat consequences go beyond connectivity. Fences prevent access to ephemeral pools and patchy reed beds that provide forage and shade in late summer, and grazing pressure has become uneven, with heavy browsing on the leased side and rank, unburned sedge on the conservation side [doc30]. The occupied habitat is smaller, more uniform, and less connected than the pre-fencing baseline.

Three remediation options are under review: 30–50 m fence setbacks with replanting of saltmarsh couch and clustered sedge; culverts and jump-outs at nine priority crossings; and a seasonal gate regime during the wet months. A stepped corridor linking South Kestrel to Lower Kestrel Flats is also proposed, using existing drainage reserves. The authors recommend adaptive monitoring against genetic and demographic thresholds before considering genetic rescue for the Osprey Sedge cluster [doc30].

## A World of Edges

The four places described here are not separate habitats so much as four expressions of the same principle. The highland scree is a gradient in elevation and substrate; the foothill grassland is a gradient maintained by fire size; the treeline ecotone is a gradient in shrub cover, mist, and time of day; the saltmarsh is a gradient in salinity, tide, and access. In each case, the kangaroo's persistence depends on the gradient remaining continuous and on the particular structure—scree, short grass, mist pocket, open sedge—remaining available.

The pressures are equally consistent. Scree is contracting upward [doc10]. Fire regimes have shifted toward fewer, larger burns [doc18]. Mist-pocket hydrology is vulnerable to drainage and climate change [doc22]. Fencing has cut a lowland population into five clusters [doc30]. None of these changes is a simple loss of area. Each is a change in the arrangement of the habitat, and it is the arrangement—the edge, the corridor, the pocket, the seasonal flush—that the animals use.

That is why the management proposals converge on gradients and connections rather than fixed zones. A corridor should protect an elevational gradient [doc10]. A grassland should be managed for patch burns rather than uniform fuel reduction [doc18]. An ecotone should be protected for its hydrology and its shrub–grass boundary [doc22]. A lowland should be reconnected at the places where movement is still possible [doc30]. The next chapters turn to how the animals use these places: what they eat, how they communicate, when they move, and how their bodies cope with the conditions described here.
