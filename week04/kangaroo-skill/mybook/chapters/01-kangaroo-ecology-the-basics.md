The Umbral Highlands are not home to a single, simple kangaroo. They hold a set of named forms whose relationships are still argued over in the literature, and whose daily lives are organised around rock, mist, and the shifting green of the high country. To describe their ecology is first to ask what kind of animal is being described. The corpus answers that question in several voices, and the disagreement is part of the material.

This chapter sets out the basics: how the animals are classified, how they group themselves, when they are active, how they move across the year, and what role they play in the places they occupy. It draws on a field notebook from the northern basalt escarpment [doc01], a study of the saltmarsh lowlands [doc13], and two taxonomic reassessments [doc17] [doc29]. Later chapters take up diet, behaviour, movement, physiology, predators, folklore, and conservation in turn.

## Naming the Animal

The field notebook from Corrin Saddle is confident about what it is watching: the Shadowtail Kangaroo, which it calls *M. umbracauda* [doc01]. The phylogenetic reassessment reaches a different name for what appears to be the same highland animal, *Umbracauda nocturna*, and argues that the dark and pale forms long treated as separate species are colour morphs of one [doc17]. The evidence it offers is genetic as well as morphological.

> Dark and pale phenotypes share the most common haplotype, show no reciprocal monophyly, and differ by at most 0.7% at cytochrome b.
> — doc17

The paper's conclusion is categorical:

> The Umbracauda group is therefore monotypic at the species level.
> — doc17

A second reassessment concerns a different animal, the Mistbound Kangaroo of the Mistbound Plateau, and reaches the opposite kind of conclusion: it argues for splitting, not lumping. Where *M. nebulosus* has been treated as a subspecies of the Crescent Kangaroo since 1987, the authors find consistent differences in body mass, skull shape, and contact calls, and no evidence of interbreeding at the contact zone [doc29]. Their recommendation is explicit:

> I recommend raising nebulosus to species rank, with the type locality restricted to the upper Wrenfall Valley.
> — doc29

Read together, the two papers show taxonomy as a working argument rather than a fixed list. One reduces two names to one species; the other raises one subspecies to a species. Neither result is presented as a final verdict, and the corpus does not reconcile the names used by the field notebook with those used by the phylogenetic study. This book therefore keeps the names each source uses and attributes them, rather than choosing a single label for the whole animal.

## Troupes and Daily Rhythms

What the animals do is clearer than what they should be called. On the basalt escarpment above Corrin Saddle, the field notebook describes social life as loose and fluid rather than tightly organised. Groups shift in composition from day to day, and the observer found no stable harem or consistent adult sex ratio [doc01].

> Group structure is best described as loose troupes of 4–7 animals.
> — doc01

Across 23 sightings between 2016 and 2019, mean troupe size was 5.4 animals, with individuals spaced 12–40 m apart while foraging. Cohesion is maintained by low postural signals rather than close following, and disturbed troupes fragment into two or three subgroups before reassembling within 20–35 minutes. Juveniles stay within about 8 m of an adult female but are not physically guarded [doc01].

The daily cycle is sharply divided. The animals are crepuscular, with movement peaks at first light and again before dusk, and a long midday rest in shaded scree crevices.

> The activity cycle is crepuscular.
> — doc01

Camera traps recorded only about 7% of detections at night, concentrated on moonlit nights. Morning feeding bouts averaged 42 minutes; evening bouts ran longer, at 55–70 minutes, before the animals moved to bedding sites. Ambient temperature at first emergence ranged from 6.8–9.4 °C in May to 11.2–14.0 °C in November [doc01]. The pattern is not merely a preference for cool hours. It is a way of using a hot, exposed landscape while avoiding the worst of its midday heat.

## A Seasonal Mosaic

The highland population does not make a simple downslope migration. Its range use follows moisture and shelter, and the direction of movement changes with the season and the aspect of the slope.

> Seasonal range shifts follow moisture and shelter rather than a simple downslope migration.
> — doc01

In the dry season, sightings concentrate at 1950–2100 m near seep lines and deep crevices, where forage retains more water. In the cool wet season, animals move to sheltered benches at 1760–1840 m, with occasional excursions lower during cold spells. GPS fixes from three collared adults moved from a mean altitude of 2040 m in February to 1810 m in July, a shift of about 230 m. Aspect use changes too: south-facing scree dominates in summer, while east-facing benches are used more in winter [doc01].

The lower boundary of this range is abrupt. At about 1785 m, where scree gives way to continuous grassland, sign drops to near zero.

> The lower boundary is abrupt: at 1785 m, where scree gives way to continuous grassland, sign drops to near zero.
> — doc01

The observer interprets this as a dependence on interstitial spaces between basalt plates for resting and escape, with open grassland used only for short crossings [doc01]. That reading makes the highland kangaroo a specialist of a particular physical structure, not simply of a particular altitude. It also explains why the animal's range can be mapped as a shifting mosaic of seep margins, winter benches, and scree above 1800 m rather than as a single elevation band.

## Kangaroos as Ecological Agents

The lowland counterpart tells a different ecological story. In the Verrin Saltmarsh Lowlands, nine to fourteen overwintering troupes of Crescent Kangaroo descend from the dry uplands each November and remain until March [doc13]. They graze the sedge meadows, wallow in intertidal mud, and move seeds across the marsh. The study argues that these animals are not passive residents of the lowland but active participants in its disturbance regime.

> We argue that overwintering troupes are ecological agents within the lowland disturbance regime, not merely resident herbivores, and that creek straightening and reduced tidal exchange threaten both the sedge mosaic and the wallows.
> — doc13

The mechanism is partly digestive. Feeding trials showed that a substantial fraction of sedge seeds survives passage through the gut and remains able to germinate.

> Feeding trials show that 38% of ingested sedge seeds remain viable after gut passage, with retention times of 19–27 hours, allowing troupes to move propagules one to four kilometres.
> — doc13

The wallows matter as well. Repeated trampling homogenises surface sediment, creates depressions that trap seeds and organic matter, and aerates the upper mud at low tide. Wallow sediments retain more moisture and have lower bulk density than adjacent mud, and they support a distinct assemblage of halotolerant invertebrates [doc13]. The kangaroo is therefore both a grazer and a modifier of the habitat it uses. Its ecology cannot be written as the animal plus its food; it has to include the mud, the seeds, and the small animals that live in the wallows.

## What the Basics Establish

Three points emerge from these sources. First, the animals are socially flexible: loose troupes, shifting membership, and spacing maintained by posture rather than hierarchy [doc01]. Second, their range use is driven by fine-grained conditions—seep lines, sheltered benches, scree structure, mist, and moisture—rather than by a single broad habitat category. Third, their ecological effect extends beyond grazing, through seed dispersal and the creation of wallow habitats [doc13].

The taxonomic disagreement is not a distraction from these points. It shapes how the animals are counted, protected, and compared. A population treated as one species with two colour morphs is a different conservation object from two species with separate ranges [doc17], and a subspecies raised to species rank changes the status of every local population [doc29]. The next chapter turns from what the kangaroos are and how they live to the places themselves: the highland scree, the fire-shaped foothills, the treeline ecotone, and the saltmarsh lowlands.
