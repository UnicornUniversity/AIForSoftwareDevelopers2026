# Type 2 Diabetes

Source: ADA Standards of Care (2024); NICE guideline NG28
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Type 2 diabetes — pathophysiology, presentation, and management outline
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of publicly available guidance; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Type 2 diabetes is characterized by a combination of insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency. It typically develops gradually over years, often preceded by a period of prediabetes (see **08_prediabetes.md**). Type 2 accounts for roughly 90% of all diabetes cases globally.

## Key information

Onset is usually insidious. Many patients are diagnosed on screening (see **04_screening.md**) rather than with classic hyperglycaemic symptoms. When symptoms are present, they include polyuria, polydipsia, fatigue, blurred vision, and slow-healing wounds or recurrent infections.

Diagnosis follows the standard criteria: FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or a random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L with symptoms (see **01_diabetes_diagnosis.md**).

Supportive features for type 2:

- Older age at onset, though increasingly seen in younger adults and adolescents.
- Overweight or obesity, particularly central adiposity (though type 2 also occurs in lean individuals, especially in some Asian populations).
- Family history of type 2 diabetes.
- Features of the metabolic syndrome (hypertension, dyslipidaemia, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease).
- Preserved C-peptide indicating retained endogenous insulin production.
- Absent pancreatic autoantibodies.
- Slow or no progression to ketoacidosis, though it can occur under severe stress.

**Management** is stepwise. Lifestyle change (dietary pattern, physical activity, weight loss) is foundational and continues throughout the disease course. Metformin is the traditional first-line pharmacotherapy in most guidelines, unless contraindicated. Second-line agents are selected based on cardiovascular and renal risk profile, weight considerations, hypoglycaemia risk, and cost. Options include SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, and eventually insulin. See **12_medications.md** for details.

Glycaemic targets are individualized. A common target is HbA1c ≤ 7.0% (53 mmol/mol), relaxed upward for older adults, those with limited life expectancy, hypoglycaemia risk, or established complications, and sometimes tightened for younger patients with a long anticipated disease course.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

Type 2 is not simply "adult-onset" diabetes. Adolescents with obesity increasingly develop type 2 diabetes, and adult-onset type 1 diabetes (LADA) is not rare (see **05_type1_diabetes.md**). Classification affects treatment, so clinical features, autoantibodies, and C-peptide are used when the picture is unclear.

Type 2 can present with a hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state (HHS), particularly in older adults with intercurrent illness. HHS features severe hyperglycaemia (often > 33 mmol/L), high effective plasma osmolality, and minimal ketosis. It is a hyperglycaemic emergency distinct from DKA (see **09_diabetic_ketoacidosis.md**).

Remission of type 2 diabetes is possible with substantial weight loss (through diet, bariatric surgery, or GLP-1 receptor agonists). Remission is typically defined as HbA1c < 6.5% sustained for at least three months without pharmacotherapy.

## Examples

A 55-year-old with BMI 32 kg/m² and hypertension has a screening HbA1c of 7.2%. Repeat testing confirms diabetes. She has no symptoms of hyperglycaemia. Lifestyle counselling is intensified and metformin is initiated. Six months later HbA1c is 6.6%.

A 62-year-old with type 2 diabetes and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease has an HbA1c of 8.1% on metformin. An SGLT2 inhibitor is added, based on cardiovascular benefit, and HbA1c improves to 7.1% over three months.
