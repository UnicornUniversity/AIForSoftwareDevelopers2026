# Gestational Diabetes

Source: ADA Standards of Care (2024); WHO *Diagnostic Criteria and Classification of Hyperglycaemia First Detected in Pregnancy* (2013)
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Gestational diabetes mellitus — screening, diagnosis, and management outline
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of publicly available guidance; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is hyperglycaemia first detected during pregnancy that does not meet the criteria for overt diabetes. It reflects the physiological insulin resistance of pregnancy exceeding the mother's compensatory capacity. Left untreated, GDM increases the risk of macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, and pre-eclampsia, and increases the mother's long-term risk of type 2 diabetes.

## Key information

**Screening.** Universal screening is recommended between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation. Women with risk factors — prior GDM, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², family history of diabetes, prior macrosomic infant, PCOS, high-risk ethnicity — should be screened at the first antenatal visit to identify pre-existing diabetes.

**Diagnostic thresholds (IADPSG / WHO 2013 one-step 75 g OGTT).** GDM is diagnosed if any one of the following is met:

- **Fasting plasma glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L (92 mg/dL)**.
- **1-hour plasma glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L (180 mg/dL)**.
- **2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L (153 mg/dL)**.

These thresholds are lower than the diagnostic thresholds for diabetes outside pregnancy (see **01_diabetes_diagnosis.md** and **03_blood_glucose.md**), because pregnancy-related complications rise at lower glucose levels than the classical macrovascular endpoints.

Some jurisdictions use a two-step approach (a 50 g glucose challenge test followed by a 100 g OGTT if positive). Thresholds differ between the two approaches; the diagnosing clinician should apply the criteria consistent with the local guideline.

**Management.** Medical nutrition therapy and physical activity are first-line. Self-monitoring of blood glucose is standard. If glycaemic targets are not met with lifestyle alone, insulin is the recommended pharmacotherapy in most guidelines; metformin is used in some. Common glycaemic targets in pregnancy are fasting < 5.3 mmol/L, 1-hour postprandial < 7.8 mmol/L, or 2-hour postprandial < 6.7 mmol/L.

**Postpartum follow-up.** All women with GDM should be reclassified after pregnancy — commonly a 75 g OGTT at 4–12 weeks postpartum — and screened for type 2 diabetes at least every three years thereafter. Approximately 50% of women with GDM develop type 2 diabetes within 10–20 years.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

GDM is not the same as pre-existing diabetes first detected in pregnancy. A woman with plasma glucose meeting non-pregnancy diabetes criteria at the first antenatal visit should be classified as having overt diabetes in pregnancy, not GDM, because the fetal risk profile and long-term implications differ.

HbA1c is not the preferred diagnostic test in pregnancy because normal HbA1c falls during pregnancy due to increased red-cell turnover, and thresholds calibrated outside pregnancy do not directly translate. Diagnosis relies on plasma glucose from the OGTT.

Diagnostic thresholds vary across guidelines. Educational materials should identify which criteria are being applied.

## Examples

A woman at 26 weeks' gestation undergoes a 75 g OGTT with values of fasting 5.4 mmol/L, 1-hour 9.8 mmol/L, 2-hour 7.6 mmol/L. The fasting value meets the IADPSG threshold for GDM. Nutrition counselling and self-monitoring are commenced.

A woman with prior GDM has a first-antenatal-visit HbA1c of 6.6%. This meets criteria for overt diabetes (not GDM) and is managed accordingly, with early referral to a multidisciplinary team.
