# Screening for Diabetes and Prediabetes

Source: ADA Standards of Care (2024); USPSTF *Screening for Prediabetes and Type 2 Diabetes in Adults* (2021)
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Population screening for type 2 diabetes and prediabetes in adults
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of screening indications; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Type 2 diabetes has a long asymptomatic phase. Screening asymptomatic adults can identify diabetes and prediabetes early, enabling lifestyle intervention or pharmacotherapy before complications develop. Screening for type 1 diabetes in the general population is not recommended; type 1 typically presents symptomatically over weeks.

## Key information

Adults meeting any of the following criteria should be considered for screening:

- Age 35 years or older (ADA); age 35–70 years with overweight or obesity (USPSTF).
- Body mass index ≥ 25 kg/m² (≥ 23 kg/m² in Asian populations) with one or more additional risk factors.
- First-degree relative with diabetes.
- High-risk race or ethnicity (South Asian, Black, Hispanic/Latino, American Indian, Pacific Islander).
- History of cardiovascular disease.
- Hypertension (≥ 140/90 mmHg or on antihypertensive therapy).
- HDL cholesterol < 0.90 mmol/L (35 mg/dL) or triglycerides > 2.82 mmol/L (250 mg/dL).
- Polycystic ovary syndrome.
- Physical inactivity.
- Prior gestational diabetes (screen lifelong at least every three years).

Acceptable screening tests are the same as the diagnostic tests: FPG, 2-hour plasma glucose during OGTT, or HbA1c. In routine screening, HbA1c is often preferred because it does not require fasting and is less influenced by acute conditions.

Frequency: if screening is normal, repeat every three years. If prediabetes is found, repeat annually.

Children and adolescents with overweight or obesity and additional risk factors should be considered for screening starting at age 10 years or the onset of puberty.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

Screening is not the same as diagnosis. An abnormal screening result must be confirmed with a repeat test on a separate day before establishing a diagnosis (see **01_diabetes_diagnosis.md**).

Pregnancy screening follows a different pathway. Women with risk factors are screened at the first antenatal visit; all women without prior diabetes are screened at 24–28 weeks gestation using an OGTT. See **07_gestational_diabetes.md**.

Screening decisions in older adults should consider life expectancy, functional status, and preferences. In a patient with limited life expectancy or advanced frailty, an incidental diagnosis of prediabetes is unlikely to translate into a meaningful benefit.

## Examples

A 42-year-old with a BMI of 28 kg/m² and a father with type 2 diabetes has a screening HbA1c of 5.9%. This meets the ADA criterion for prediabetes. She is offered lifestyle intervention and a repeat HbA1c in one year.

A 60-year-old with hypertension is screened with an FPG of 6.8 mmol/L. This is in the impaired-fasting-glucose range. An OGTT is arranged, giving a 2-hour value of 8.9 mmol/L (impaired glucose tolerance). Both tests independently support prediabetes.

A woman with a history of gestational diabetes has annual FPG measurements. At 45 years of age her FPG is 7.1 mmol/L. A repeat test confirms diabetes. Prior gestational diabetes is a strong risk factor for later type 2 diabetes; lifelong screening is warranted (see **07_gestational_diabetes.md**).
