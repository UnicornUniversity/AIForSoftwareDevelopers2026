# Hypoglycaemia

Source: ADA Standards of Care (2024); International Hypoglycaemia Study Group consensus (2017)
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Hypoglycaemia in diabetes — classification, recognition, and management outline
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of publicly available guidance; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Hypoglycaemia is the most common acute complication of diabetes treatment and the principal factor limiting how aggressively glycaemia can be controlled. It is most frequent in people using insulin or insulin secretagogues (sulfonylureas, meglitinides). Prevention, early recognition, and appropriate treatment are core self-management skills.

## Key information

**Classification (International Hypoglycaemia Study Group).**

- **Level 1 — Glucose alert value**: plasma glucose < 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL). Not necessarily symptomatic; a threshold for action to avoid a drop into clinically important hypoglycaemia.
- **Level 2 — Clinically significant hypoglycaemia**: plasma glucose < 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL). Low enough to indicate serious, clinically important hypoglycaemia.
- **Level 3 — Severe hypoglycaemia**: hypoglycaemia associated with cognitive impairment sufficient to require external assistance. No specific glucose threshold; severity is defined by the need for help.

**Symptoms** are usually classified as autonomic (sweating, palpitations, tremor, hunger, anxiety) or neuroglycopenic (confusion, drowsiness, difficulty speaking, incoordination, seizures, loss of consciousness).

**Treatment.**

- **Conscious, able to swallow.** 15–20 g of fast-acting carbohydrate: for example, four to five glucose tablets, 150–200 mL of a sugar-containing (non-diet) drink, or a tablespoon of honey. Recheck glucose after 15 minutes; repeat if still low. Follow with a longer-acting carbohydrate snack or the next scheduled meal to prevent recurrence.
- **Impaired consciousness or unable to swallow.** Intramuscular or subcutaneous glucagon 1 mg (for adults); nasal glucagon 3 mg is an alternative where available. In a hospital or emergency service setting, intravenous 10% or 20% dextrose is given.

**Hypoglycaemia unawareness** is the loss of the autonomic warning symptoms that normally precede neuroglycopenia. It is a major risk factor for severe hypoglycaemia. It can develop after repeated hypoglycaemic episodes and can be partially reversed by strictly avoiding hypoglycaemia for two to three weeks.

**Nocturnal hypoglycaemia** is under-recognized; symptoms may be absent, with only morning headache, unrefreshing sleep, or elevated morning glucose (rebound) as clues. Continuous glucose monitoring greatly increases detection.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

Not all low glucose values are equal. A single reading of 3.7 mmol/L on a person with tight glycaemic control differs from repeated level-2 or level-3 events. Recurring or severe hypoglycaemia should prompt review of insulin regimen, carbohydrate patterns, alcohol intake, and physical activity.

Patients on sulfonylureas may have prolonged hypoglycaemia because these drugs have long durations of action; observation after treatment of a hypoglycaemic event on a sulfonylurea is warranted (see **12_medications.md**).

Medications for diabetes vary in hypoglycaemia risk. Metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists carry low intrinsic hypoglycaemia risk. Sulfonylureas, meglitinides, and insulin carry substantial risk.

Hypoglycaemia can affect glycaemic targets. In older adults, those with frailty, established cardiovascular disease, or hypoglycaemia unawareness, individualized targets often relax HbA1c targets to reduce hypoglycaemia risk.

## Examples

A 72-year-old on a sulfonylurea and metformin becomes confused and diaphoretic; a bystander tests capillary glucose at 2.6 mmol/L. Oral glucose tablets restore consciousness; the sulfonylurea is later switched given the age and hypoglycaemia risk.

A 28-year-old with type 1 diabetes on multiple daily injections has repeated CGM alerts overnight below 3.9 mmol/L. There are no daytime symptoms. The basal insulin dose is reduced and CGM is used to verify resolution.

A 16-year-old with type 1 diabetes has a seizure at school. Blood glucose is 1.8 mmol/L. Intramuscular glucagon is given by a trained staff member; consciousness returns within 10 minutes. Emergency services transport the patient; the school reviews its hypoglycaemia protocol.
