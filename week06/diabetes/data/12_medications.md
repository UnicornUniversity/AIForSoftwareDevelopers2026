# Medications for Diabetes

Source: ADA Standards of Care (2024); NICE guideline NG28
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Overview of glucose-lowering medication classes used in diabetes
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of publicly available guidance; not a clinical decision tool. Not a prescribing reference.
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Medications for diabetes are chosen based on the type of diabetes, the individual's cardiovascular and renal risk, weight considerations, hypoglycaemia risk, side-effect profile, and cost. Type 1 diabetes requires insulin from diagnosis and for life. Type 2 diabetes is typically managed with a stepwise combination of lifestyle change and one or more non-insulin therapies, with insulin added when glycaemic targets are not achieved otherwise.

## Key information

**Metformin.** A biguanide; enhances insulin sensitivity and reduces hepatic glucose production. Traditional first-line agent for type 2 diabetes unless contraindicated. Weight-neutral, low hypoglycaemia risk, inexpensive. Common side effects: gastrointestinal upset (usually transient). Contraindicated at low estimated GFR; the exact threshold depends on the regional guideline (commonly avoided below eGFR 30 mL/min/1.73 m²).

**SGLT2 inhibitors** (e.g. empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin). Reduce renal glucose reabsorption; lower HbA1c modestly and produce weight loss. Cardiovascular protection in established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, kidney protection in chronic kidney disease, and heart-failure protection are class effects for multiple agents (see **11_diabetes_complications.md**). Low intrinsic hypoglycaemia risk. Notable adverse effects: genital mycotic infections; euglycaemic DKA (see **09_diabetic_ketoacidosis.md**); rare Fournier gangrene.

**GLP-1 receptor agonists** (e.g. liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide). Injectable (semaglutide is also available orally); mimic incretin action to enhance insulin secretion and slow gastric emptying. Lower HbA1c substantially and produce significant weight loss. Cardiovascular protection in established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease is a class effect for several agents. Low intrinsic hypoglycaemia risk. Common adverse effects: nausea, vomiting (usually transient with dose titration). Rare: pancreatitis. Some agents are contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2.

**DPP-4 inhibitors** (e.g. sitagliptin, linagliptin). Oral; modest HbA1c lowering, weight-neutral, low hypoglycaemia risk. Fewer cardiovascular or renal outcome benefits than SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists. Well tolerated.

**Sulfonylureas** (e.g. gliclazide, glimepiride). Stimulate insulin secretion; effective HbA1c lowering; inexpensive. Significant hypoglycaemia risk (see **10_hypoglycemia.md**) and weight gain. Considered when cost is a primary consideration and hypoglycaemia risk is acceptable.

**Thiazolidinediones** (e.g. pioglitazone). Improve insulin sensitivity. Effective HbA1c lowering; low hypoglycaemia risk. Notable adverse effects: fluid retention, heart failure risk, weight gain, increased fracture risk in some populations.

**Insulin.** Required in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2 when other agents are insufficient. Basal insulins (glargine, detemir, degludec) provide 24-hour coverage; rapid-acting insulins (lispro, aspart, glulisine) are used with meals. Hypoglycaemia and weight gain are the principal adverse effects.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

Choice of second-line therapy in type 2 diabetes is now guided as much by cardiovascular and renal indications as by HbA1c. In established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist with proven benefit is often preferred regardless of baseline HbA1c. In chronic kidney disease or heart failure, an SGLT2 inhibitor is often preferred.

Insulin secretagogues (sulfonylureas, meglitinides) are unique among the newer options in carrying substantial hypoglycaemia risk; hypoglycaemia may be prolonged. Alcohol, missed meals, renal impairment, and older age amplify the risk.

Some agents require dose adjustment or should be avoided at reduced eGFR. Regional prescribing guidance should be consulted.

## Examples

A 55-year-old with type 2 diabetes, HbA1c 8.4%, BMI 34 kg/m², and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease begins metformin and is escalated with a GLP-1 receptor agonist. Over six months, HbA1c falls to 6.9% and weight declines by 6 kg.

A 70-year-old with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is started on an SGLT2 inhibitor for both cardiovascular protection and modest glycaemic benefit; metformin is continued.

A frail 82-year-old with type 2 diabetes on a sulfonylurea and metformin has repeated hypoglycaemic events. The sulfonylurea is stopped; a DPP-4 inhibitor is added; the HbA1c target is relaxed to 7.5%–8.0%.
