# Blood Glucose Measurement

Source: WHO *Definition and Diagnosis of Diabetes Mellitus and Intermediate Hyperglycaemia* (2006); ADA Standards of Care (2024)
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Fasting, postprandial, and random plasma glucose measurement
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of laboratory glucose testing; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Plasma glucose is the reference measurement for glycaemic status. Three commonly used tests are: fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-hour plasma glucose during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and random plasma glucose. Each answers a different question and has different interpretation.

## Key information

**Fasting plasma glucose (FPG).** Blood is drawn after at least eight hours without caloric intake. Interpretation:

- **< 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL)** — normal.
- **5.6–6.9 mmol/L (100–125 mg/dL)** — impaired fasting glucose (prediabetes).
- **≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL)** — provisional diabetes diagnosis, requires confirmation.

**Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT).** After an overnight fast, the patient ingests 75 g of anhydrous glucose in 250–300 mL of water; plasma glucose is measured at 0 and 120 minutes. The 2-hour value is interpreted:

- **< 7.8 mmol/L (140 mg/dL)** — normal glucose tolerance.
- **7.8–11.0 mmol/L (140–199 mg/dL)** — impaired glucose tolerance (prediabetes).
- **≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL)** — provisional diabetes diagnosis, requires confirmation.

**Random plasma glucose.** Measured without regard to time since last meal. A value ≥ 11.1 mmol/L in a symptomatic patient supports a diabetes diagnosis without further testing.

Plasma glucose values are the reference. Whole-blood values from capillary meters run approximately 10–15% lower than plasma. Point-of-care meters are calibrated to display plasma-equivalent values but remain unsuitable for diagnosis.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

The three tests do not always agree. It is common for a patient to meet criteria on one test but not another: OGTT is more sensitive than FPG and detects more cases of impaired glucose tolerance; FPG is more reproducible. When results disagree, the test that was abnormal should be repeated for confirmation before establishing the diagnosis.

Acute intercurrent illness, corticosteroid administration, and recent surgery all raise plasma glucose transiently. A single abnormal value in this context should not be used to diagnose diabetes; retest after recovery.

Capillary glucose testing has an important role in monitoring, particularly for people using insulin, but not in diagnosis. In-hospital point-of-care glucose is essential for detecting and managing hyperglycaemic crises.

## Examples

A patient with an FPG of 7.4 mmol/L and no symptoms is asked to return for a repeat test. The repeat FPG is 7.1 mmol/L, confirming diabetes.

A patient with impaired fasting glucose (FPG 6.3 mmol/L) undergoes an OGTT. The 2-hour value is 12.2 mmol/L, providing evidence of diabetes on the OGTT criterion. This is confirmed on a second day.

A patient with pneumonia has a random plasma glucose of 13 mmol/L. The value is not by itself diagnostic — infection can transiently elevate glucose. Follow-up testing after recovery is required.
