# Chronic Complications of Diabetes

Source: ADA Standards of Care (2024); NICE guidelines NG28, NG17
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Microvascular and macrovascular complications of diabetes
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of publicly available guidance; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Long-standing hyperglycaemia damages small and large blood vessels and their target organs. Diabetes-related complications are grouped as microvascular (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and macrovascular (coronary, cerebrovascular, and peripheral arterial disease). Prevention and early detection depend on regular structured review.

## Key information

**Microvascular complications.**

- **Diabetic retinopathy.** The commonest microvascular complication and a leading cause of preventable blindness in working-age adults. Annual dilated fundus examination or retinal photography is standard. Progression is reduced by glycaemic and blood pressure control. Proliferative retinopathy or diabetic macular oedema requires specialist ophthalmological treatment (laser, anti-VEGF injections).

- **Diabetic nephropathy.** Marked by rising albuminuria (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 3 mg/mmol) and/or falling estimated GFR. Annual measurement of urine ACR and eGFR is standard. Renin-angiotensin system blockade (ACE inhibitor or ARB) and, more recently, SGLT2 inhibitors and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists reduce progression.

- **Diabetic neuropathy.** The most common form is a distal symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy, presenting with numbness, tingling, or burning pain in the feet. Autonomic neuropathy affects cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary function. Annual foot examination for sensation (10 g monofilament, vibration), pulses, and skin integrity is standard. Neuropathic pain may be managed with agents such as duloxetine, pregabalin, gabapentin, or amitriptyline.

**Macrovascular complications.** Diabetes approximately doubles the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Blood pressure control (typically < 130/80 mmHg with individualization), statin therapy for cardiovascular risk reduction, smoking cessation, and consideration of antiplatelet therapy in established disease are cornerstones. In established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or heart failure, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists provide additional cardiovascular protection independent of glycaemic effect (see **12_medications.md**).

**Diabetic foot.** The intersection of neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, and infection risk. Foot ulceration and amputation are largely preventable through structured screening, patient education, appropriate footwear, and prompt referral for high-risk feet or active ulceration.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

Complication risk is not driven by glycaemia alone. Blood pressure control, lipid management, smoking status, and albuminuria all contribute independently. Focusing solely on HbA1c misses much of the modifiable risk.

Not every symptom in a person with diabetes is due to diabetes. Neuropathy has other causes (vitamin B12 deficiency, alcohol, chemotherapy, hereditary conditions), and these should be considered when the presentation is atypical.

Complications can be present at diagnosis of type 2 diabetes because of the long asymptomatic phase. A baseline complications review at diagnosis — retinal photography, urine ACR, eGFR, foot examination, cardiovascular risk assessment — is standard.

## Examples

A 60-year-old with type 2 diabetes for eight years has a urine ACR of 5.2 mg/mmol and eGFR 62 mL/min/1.73 m². This is early diabetic kidney disease. An ACE inhibitor is titrated and an SGLT2 inhibitor is added, guided by current renal thresholds.

A 55-year-old with type 2 diabetes complains of burning pain in both feet at night. Neurological examination shows reduced monofilament sensation to the metatarsal heads bilaterally. This is symptomatic diabetic peripheral neuropathy; foot-care advice is reinforced, glycaemic control reviewed, and duloxetine is offered for pain.

A 68-year-old with type 2 diabetes and prior myocardial infarction has HbA1c 7.4% on metformin. A GLP-1 receptor agonist is added for cardiovascular benefit and further glycaemic improvement.
