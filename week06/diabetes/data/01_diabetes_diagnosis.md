# Diabetes Diagnosis

Source: WHO Classification of Diabetes Mellitus (2019); ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2024)
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Diagnostic criteria for diabetes mellitus
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of publicly available diagnostic thresholds; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Diabetes mellitus is diagnosed by demonstrating elevated blood glucose using one of four accepted laboratory tests: fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-hour plasma glucose during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), or a random plasma glucose in the presence of classic symptoms. Any one abnormal result should normally be confirmed by repeat testing on a separate day, unless the patient is unequivocally symptomatic with a random glucose value above the diagnostic threshold.

## Key information

The four diagnostic pathways use the following thresholds, aligned across WHO and ADA guidance:

- **Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL)** after at least eight hours without caloric intake.
- **2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL)** during a standardized 75 g oral glucose tolerance test.
- **HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol)** using a laboratory method certified by the NGSP and standardized to the DCCT assay.
- **Random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL)** in a patient with classic hyperglycaemic symptoms (polyuria, polydipsia, unexplained weight loss) or a hyperglycaemic crisis.

Two abnormal results — either the same test repeated, or two different tests from a single sample — are considered confirmatory.

Diabetes should not be diagnosed on the basis of a single abnormal test in an asymptomatic person. Point-of-care devices, including capillary glucose meters and point-of-care HbA1c analyzers, are not accepted for diagnostic use; they are appropriate for monitoring only.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

HbA1c is not reliable for diagnosis in several situations: pregnancy, recent significant blood loss or transfusion, haemolytic anaemia, chronic kidney disease at advanced stages, certain haemoglobinopathies, and after recent administration of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents. In these settings, diagnosis should rely on plasma glucose.

Diagnostic thresholds are not clinical action thresholds. Values at or just above a diagnostic cutoff establish diabetes but do not by themselves dictate therapy intensity. Values below the diabetes cutoff but above the normal range indicate prediabetes (see **08_prediabetes.md**).

Gestational diabetes uses different thresholds and is diagnosed by an OGTT performed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation; see **07_gestational_diabetes.md**.

## Examples

A 54-year-old patient with polyuria, polydipsia, and unexplained weight loss presents with a random plasma glucose of 14.8 mmol/L. Classical symptoms plus a random glucose above 11.1 mmol/L are diagnostic; no repeat test is required.

An asymptomatic 47-year-old has an HbA1c of 6.8% at a routine health check. Because the patient is asymptomatic, a second confirmatory test is required. A repeat HbA1c of 6.7% one week later confirms diabetes. Note that 6.8% is only 0.3 percentage points above the diagnostic cutoff and would not warrant an aggressive treatment escalation on the basis of the value alone.

An asymptomatic patient has an FPG of 7.2 mmol/L but an HbA1c of 6.1%. This discordance is common. A repeat FPG on a separate day confirms diabetes if it remains ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; if not, the patient meets criteria for prediabetes.
