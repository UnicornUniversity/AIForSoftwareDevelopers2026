# Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

Source: ADA/EASD *Hyperglycemic Crises in Adults with Diabetes: A Consensus Report* (2024); NICE guideline NG18
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Diabetic ketoacidosis — recognition, diagnosis, and management outline
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of publicly available guidance; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is an acute, potentially life-threatening complication of diabetes characterized by hyperglycaemia, ketonaemia, and metabolic acidosis. It is most common in type 1 diabetes but occurs in type 2 diabetes as well, particularly under precipitating stress such as infection or missed insulin. Early recognition and prompt hospital treatment are essential.

## Key information

**Warning signs and symptoms.** Onset over hours to a few days, typically featuring:

- Polyuria, polydipsia, and dehydration.
- Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain.
- Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid respirations) and a "fruity" (acetone) breath odour.
- Fatigue, weakness, altered mental status ranging from drowsiness to coma.
- Recent hyperglycaemic symptoms if not previously diagnosed with diabetes.

In children, DKA may present as the first manifestation of type 1 diabetes; abdominal pain and vomiting can be misattributed to gastroenteritis.

**Biochemical definition.** Classical DKA requires all three of:

- **Hyperglycaemia**: plasma glucose > 11.0 mmol/L (200 mg/dL). Note: *euglycaemic DKA* — plasma glucose < 11.0 mmol/L — occurs particularly with SGLT2 inhibitor use, in pregnancy, and after prolonged fasting.
- **Ketonaemia**: blood beta-hydroxybutyrate ≥ 3.0 mmol/L, or urine ketones 2+ or greater.
- **Metabolic acidosis**: venous bicarbonate < 15 mmol/L and/or venous pH < 7.3.

Severity is graded by the degree of acidosis and clinical features:

- **Mild**: pH 7.25–7.30, bicarbonate 10–15 mmol/L, alert.
- **Moderate**: pH 7.00–7.24, bicarbonate 5–9 mmol/L, drowsy.
- **Severe**: pH < 7.00, bicarbonate < 5 mmol/L, stuporose or comatose.

**Immediate management (hospital setting).** Fluid resuscitation with intravenous saline, intravenous insulin infusion, potassium replacement guided by frequent monitoring, and treatment of the precipitating cause (infection, missed insulin, new-onset diabetes, MI, pancreatitis, drugs). Bicarbonate is not routinely recommended. Regular reassessment of glucose, ketones, electrolytes, and acid-base status guides therapy adjustments.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

DKA is not the same as hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state (HHS). HHS features severe hyperglycaemia (often > 33 mmol/L), high effective plasma osmolality (> 320 mOsm/kg), and minimal ketosis (see **06_type2_diabetes.md**). HHS is more common in older adults with type 2 diabetes and has different management priorities, though the two syndromes can overlap.

Euglycaemic DKA is easy to miss because the glucose is not markedly elevated. Any patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor presenting with vomiting, dehydration, or acidosis should have ketones and bicarbonate checked (see **12_medications.md**).

DKA at presentation is not confined to type 1 diabetes. Ketosis-prone type 2 diabetes and DKA precipitated by intercurrent illness are recognised. In every case, DKA warrants urgent evaluation for the precipitating cause.

## Examples

A 16-year-old with recent-onset polyuria and polydipsia presents with vomiting, abdominal pain, and Kussmaul respirations. Plasma glucose is 26 mmol/L, blood beta-hydroxybutyrate 5.1 mmol/L, venous pH 7.15, bicarbonate 6 mmol/L. This is severe DKA in new-onset type 1 diabetes.

A 62-year-old with type 2 diabetes on an SGLT2 inhibitor presents with two days of nausea and lethargy after a viral illness. Plasma glucose is 9.4 mmol/L (deceptively low), but beta-hydroxybutyrate is 3.8 mmol/L and venous pH is 7.22. This is euglycaemic DKA precipitated by illness and SGLT2 inhibitor use; the drug is withheld and standard DKA treatment initiated.
