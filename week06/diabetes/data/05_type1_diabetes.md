# Type 1 Diabetes

Source: ADA Standards of Care (2024); WHO *Classification of Diabetes Mellitus* (2019)
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: Type 1 diabetes — pathophysiology, presentation, and management outline
Educational adaptation: Concise summary of publicly available guidance; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

Type 1 diabetes is characterized by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells and consequent near-total insulin deficiency. It typically presents in childhood or early adulthood, but can occur at any age. Type 1 accounts for roughly 5–10% of all diabetes cases globally.

## Key information

Presentation is often over days to a few weeks with:

- **Polyuria** (excessive urination) and **polydipsia** (excessive thirst).
- **Unexplained weight loss** despite increased appetite.
- **Fatigue** and blurred vision.
- Progression to **diabetic ketoacidosis** (see **09_diabetic_ketoacidosis.md**) in a substantial minority of new presentations, particularly in children.

Diagnosis follows the standard criteria (see **01_diabetes_diagnosis.md**): FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or a random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L with classic symptoms. What distinguishes type 1 from type 2 is the underlying pathophysiology, not the diagnostic test.

Supportive features for type 1:

- Young age at onset (although type 1 can present in adulthood — sometimes termed *latent autoimmune diabetes in adults*, LADA).
- Rapid onset with pronounced symptoms.
- Absent or low C-peptide indicating insulin deficiency.
- Presence of pancreatic autoantibodies (GAD, IA-2, ZnT8, insulin autoantibodies).
- Ketosis or ketoacidosis at presentation.
- Lean body habitus (though increasingly not, as obesity becomes more prevalent).

**Management** requires exogenous insulin from diagnosis and for life. Insulin regimens are typically multiple daily injections (a long-acting basal insulin plus rapid-acting insulin with meals) or continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (an insulin pump). Carbohydrate counting is standard. Continuous glucose monitoring is increasingly the norm in many health systems.

Glycaemic targets in type 1 balance HbA1c against hypoglycaemia risk. An HbA1c of 7.0% (53 mmol/mol) or less is commonly cited for most adults with type 1, individualized upward in the presence of severe hypoglycaemia, limited life expectancy, or hypoglycaemia unawareness.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

**Type 1 must not be confused with type 2.** The management differs fundamentally: type 1 requires insulin from diagnosis; type 2 is initially managed with lifestyle change and non-insulin therapies. Misclassification, especially of adult-onset type 1 as type 2, can delay insulin initiation and precipitate ketoacidosis.

Not every young person presenting with hyperglycaemia has type 1. Monogenic diabetes (MODY), type 2 diabetes in adolescents, and secondary diabetes should be considered.

Autoantibody testing may be negative in a minority of true type 1 cases, particularly with longer disease duration. A composite clinical picture (age, body habitus, ketosis, C-peptide) is often used.

## Examples

A 14-year-old presents to the emergency department with a two-week history of polyuria, polydipsia, and 6 kg unintended weight loss. Random plasma glucose is 22 mmol/L; venous pH is 7.28; blood ketones are 4.2 mmol/L. Symptoms plus glucose confirm diabetes; the ketosis and acidosis confirm diabetic ketoacidosis. Type 1 is the presumed diagnosis and is later supported by positive GAD antibodies and low C-peptide.

A 34-year-old lean adult is diagnosed with diabetes at an HbA1c of 8.1%. Metformin is started. Over six months, glycaemia deteriorates despite adherence, and modest weight loss is noted. C-peptide is low; GAD antibodies are positive. The diagnosis is revised to latent autoimmune diabetes in adults, and basal insulin is initiated.
