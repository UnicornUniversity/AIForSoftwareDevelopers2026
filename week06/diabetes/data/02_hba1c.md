# HbA1c (Glycated Haemoglobin)

Source: WHO *Use of Glycated Haemoglobin (HbA1c) in the Diagnosis of Diabetes Mellitus* (2011); ADA Standards of Care (2024)
Source type: Educational adaptation of clinical guidelines
Topic: HbA1c measurement, interpretation, and limitations
Educational adaptation: Summary of publicly available laboratory guidance; not a clinical decision tool
Last reviewed: 2024-01

## Overview

HbA1c reflects the fraction of haemoglobin molecules that have been irreversibly glycated by circulating glucose. Because red blood cells live approximately 120 days, HbA1c integrates glucose exposure over roughly the preceding 8 to 12 weeks, with more recent weeks contributing more heavily. It is reported either as a percentage (NGSP/DCCT units) or in mmol/mol (IFCC units). A value of 6.5% corresponds to 48 mmol/mol.

## Key information

Interpretation ranges commonly used in clinical education:

- **Below 5.7% (39 mmol/mol)** — considered within the normal range for a non-diabetic adult.
- **5.7%–6.4% (39–46 mmol/mol)** — prediabetes range (ADA); WHO uses the term "intermediate hyperglycaemia" and does not endorse a single HbA1c range for it.
- **≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol)** — diagnostic threshold for diabetes when confirmed on a second occasion.

A common approximate mapping from HbA1c to average plasma glucose is:

- 6.0% ≈ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL)
- 7.0% ≈ 8.6 mmol/L (154 mg/dL)
- 8.0% ≈ 10.2 mmol/L (183 mg/dL)
- 9.0% ≈ 11.8 mmol/L (212 mg/dL)

These are population averages. Individual patients may show substantially different mean glucose for the same HbA1c.

For monitoring, HbA1c is typically measured every 3 to 6 months. A stable value in a well-controlled patient may be measured less frequently; a value that is changing, or a therapy change, warrants earlier retesting.

## Important distinctions / exceptions

Several factors can shift HbA1c away from what would be predicted from mean plasma glucose. HbA1c may be **falsely low** in conditions that shorten red-cell lifespan or increase red-cell turnover: haemolytic anaemia, recent blood loss or transfusion, pregnancy (later trimesters), erythropoiesis-stimulating agent therapy, and some haemoglobin variants. HbA1c may be **falsely high** in conditions that lengthen red-cell lifespan: iron-deficiency anaemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, splenectomy, and some haemoglobin variants. Certain haemoglobinopathies interfere with specific assay methods; laboratories should note which method they use.

HbA1c does not capture glycaemic variability. Two patients with the same HbA1c may have very different day-to-day glucose profiles — for example, one running steadily near the mean and another oscillating between hypoglycaemia and marked hyperglycaemia. Continuous glucose monitoring adds this dimension.

For diagnosis, HbA1c must be measured on a laboratory analyzer using an NGSP-certified, DCCT-aligned method. Point-of-care HbA1c is acceptable for monitoring in some jurisdictions but not for diagnosis.

## Examples

An asymptomatic adult has an HbA1c of 6.8%. This is above the 6.5% diabetes threshold. Before acting on the value, consider: was the sample processed by an accredited laboratory? Is the patient known to have any red-cell condition that would distort HbA1c? Repeat the test on a separate day before establishing the diagnosis (see **01_diabetes_diagnosis.md**).

A patient with iron-deficiency anaemia has an HbA1c of 6.7%. After iron replacement, the repeat HbA1c is 6.1%. The initial value overstated glycaemia; treating the anaemia unmasked the true value.

A patient's HbA1c is 7.0% but their continuous glucose monitor shows time-in-range (3.9–10.0 mmol/L) of only 45%, with frequent nocturnal hypoglycaemia. The HbA1c looks acceptable in isolation; the CGM profile changes the management plan.
