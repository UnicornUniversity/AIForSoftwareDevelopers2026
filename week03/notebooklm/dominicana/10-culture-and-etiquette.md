# Dominican Republic — Culture, Etiquette, Holidays, Events

Cultural context and etiquette that will make your Dominican vacation richer — and prevent the occasional awkward moment. This document also lists public holidays and festivals that can affect travel.

---

## The Dominican character

Dominicans are broadly recognized as among the warmest, most sociable people in the Americas. Culture emphasizes:
- **Family and community** — extended family networks are strong; Sundays are usually reserved for family gatherings.
- **Music and dance** — merengue and bachata are woven into daily life. Music is loud, often, and everywhere.
- **Baseball** — the national obsession. The MLB has more Dominican players per capita than any other country.
- **Direct but polite communication** — Dominicans speak plainly and value friendliness. Sarcasm and cold politeness are not the local style.
- **Time flexibility** — punctuality is looser than in Northern Europe or East Asia. "Ahorita" (right now) can mean anything from 5 minutes to 2 hours.

## Language basics and useful phrases

Dominican Spanish has some quirks: the letter "s" often drops at ends of syllables ("¿Cómo etá?" for "¿Cómo estás?"), and there is a rich local slang.

Useful greetings and courtesies:
- **Hola / Buenos días / Buenas tardes / Buenas noches** — hi / good morning / afternoon / evening.
- **¿Cómo está?** (formal) / **¿Cómo estás?** (informal) — how are you?
- **Bien, gracias, ¿y usted?** — well, thanks, and you?
- **Mucho gusto** — pleased to meet you.
- **Por favor / Gracias / De nada** — please / thank you / you're welcome.
- **Con permiso / Perdón / Disculpe** — excuse me / sorry / pardon me.
- **La cuenta, por favor** — the bill, please.
- **Buen provecho** — enjoy your meal (say it if you pass someone eating).
- **Salud** — cheers / bless you.
- **¿Cuánto cuesta?** — how much?
- **¿Dónde está...?** — where is...?
- **No entiendo** — I don't understand.
- **¿Habla inglés?** — do you speak English?

Dominican slang to recognize:
- **Chévere / Bacano** — cool / awesome.
- **Vaina** — thing/stuff (very common, mildly informal).
- **Qué lo qué** — "what's up?" (very informal).
- **Diablo** / **Diantre** — mild expression of surprise.
- **Tígre** — a savvy street-smart guy.
- **Guagua** — bus.
- **Concho** — shared taxi.
- **Un chin** — a little bit.

## Etiquette and social norms

### Greetings
- A single **kiss on the right cheek** between women, and between women and men, when introduced socially.
- Men shake hands with men; a hug ("abrazo") among friends.
- Say **"Hola, buenos días"** when entering a shop, restaurant, or taxi. Silence is considered cold.
- Address strangers with **Señor / Señora** — polite formality goes a long way.

### At the table
- Wait for **"Buen provecho"** before starting.
- Keep both hands (but not elbows) on the table.
- The eldest, host, or most senior person is usually served first.
- If you finish first, wait for others before leaving.

### Dress code
- **Resorts and beaches:** anything goes. Nudity is prohibited outside a very small number of specific beaches.
- **Casual town wandering:** shorts and t-shirts fine; **avoid swimwear off the beach**.
- **Restaurants (mid-range and up):** smart casual. Long trousers and closed shoes for men in nicer places.
- **Churches:** cover shoulders and knees.
- **Business meetings:** neat and conservative.
- **Casinos:** smart casual required.

### Photography
- Ask before photographing people, especially in rural or Haitian-community contexts.
- Do not photograph police, military, or airport-security areas.
- Some sites (churches, museums) prohibit flash.
- Buying a small item from a market vendor before photographing them is a good gesture.

### Political sensitivity
- **The relationship with Haiti** is complex. Avoid opinionated remarks on Haiti, migration, and the border. Locals may express strong views; a foreign visitor's role is to listen, not to preach.
- **Trujillo era** (1930–1961): a dark period of dictatorship still discussed. Handle sensitively.

### Religion
- Predominantly **Roman Catholic**, with a large and growing **Evangelical Protestant** community, plus **Haitian-influenced Vodou** in some rural areas.
- Sunday morning is quieter; many restaurants and businesses open later.

### LGBTQ+ travel
- Homosexuality is legal.
- Punta Cana, Sosúa, Santo Domingo Zona Colonial, and Cabarete are visibly LGBTQ-friendly.
- Public displays of affection between same-sex couples may draw stares outside tourist zones. Discretion advised in traditional/rural areas.
- Same-sex marriage is not recognized.

## Music and dance

### Merengue
The rhythmic 2/4 dance-music that defines Dominican identity. **UNESCO Intangible Heritage since 2016.** Every wedding, birthday, and Sunday BBQ has merengue playing. Iconic artists: **Juan Luis Guerra**, **Wilfrido Vargas**, **Fernando Villalona**, **Sergio Vargas**.

### Bachata
Slower, guitar-based, romantic. **UNESCO Intangible Heritage since 2019.** Originated in the rural north. Iconic artists: **Anthony Santos**, **Aventura**, **Prince Royce**, **Romeo Santos**.

### Dembow
A newer local variant of reggaeton — high-energy, urban.

### Where to hear live music
- **Zona Colonial** (Santo Domingo): weekend nights in Plaza de España.
- **Malecón** (Santo Domingo): bars and clubs along the seafront.
- **Cabarete beach bars**: live merengue/bachata most nights.
- **Las Terrenas El Pueblo de los Pescadores**: intimate live nights.
- **Boca Chica** and **Juan Dolio**: beach-club dance parties.

Free **dance lessons** are common at all-inclusive resorts. Take one — it makes everything more fun.

## Sports

### Baseball
- **Liga Dominicana de Béisbol Profesional (LIDOM):** Six teams; season October–January; championship (Serie Final) January–February; winner represents DR in the Caribbean Series.
- **Teams:** Águilas Cibaeñas (Santiago), Tigres del Licey (Santo Domingo), Toros del Este (La Romana), Estrellas Orientales (San Pedro), Leones del Escogido (Santo Domingo), Gigantes del Cibao (San Francisco).
- **Tickets:** US$5–30. Games are LOUD, festive, and cheap — a cultural experience even for non-fans.

### Boxing, cockfighting, dominoes
Also popular. Domino play is a daily social ritual — you'll see men (and increasingly women) playing on street corners and beaches.

On holidays:
- Government offices, banks, and many local businesses close.
- **Resorts operate normally.**
- **Public beaches get crowded** with domestic tourists.
- **Bus schedules reduce.**
- Alcohol sales in supermarkets can be restricted (varies by holiday).

## Festivals and events worth planning around

### Carnaval — February (weekends) and February 27
The largest cultural celebration of the year. Every town has its version, but the most spectacular are:
- **La Vega Carnaval** — the most famous, with elaborate "diablos cojuelos" masks.
- **Santo Domingo Carnaval** — closing parade on the Malecón, late February.
- **Santiago Carnaval** — famous for "lechones" and "pepines" (two rival mask styles).
- **Cabral (Barahona)** — one of the oldest, with a unique "cachúa" tradition.

### Puerto Plata Merengue Festival — early October
A week of free live merengue on the Malecón.

### Santo Domingo Merengue Festival — late July / early August
Ten days of concerts along the Malecón.

### Master of the Ocean (Cabarete) — late February
Multi-discipline watersports championship (kite, wind, surf, SUP).

### Dominican Republic Jazz Festival — early November
Puerto Plata, Sosúa, Cabarete. World-class jazz on the beach.

### Bachata Festival (Santo Domingo) — August
Major artists on outdoor stages.

### Semana Santa (Holy Week) — March or April
The country's biggest domestic-travel week. Beaches are packed. **Resort prices rise 20–40%.** Excellent atmosphere but book everything in advance.

### Larimar Fest (Barahona) — November
Regional celebration of the local blue stone.

### Christmas–New Year season
Music everywhere, decorations lavish, family gatherings central. **The country's high-price peak week.**

## Souvenirs and shopping

Signature Dominican souvenirs:
- **Larimar jewelry** — pale blue pectolite, unique to the DR. Buy in Barahona region if possible, or at reputable jewelers in Zona Colonial.
- **Amber** — Dominican amber is world-famous; the "mosquito-in-amber" that inspired Jurassic Park is here. Look for blue amber, the rarest kind.
- **Cigars** — Dominican cigars compete with Cuban. La Aurora, Arturo Fuente, Davidoff, Romeo y Julieta all produced here.
- **Rum** — Brugal 1888, Barceló Imperial, Bermúdez Reserva.
- **Cacao and chocolate** — Kah Kow bars, Chocal (women's co-op).
- **Mamajuana bottles** (pre-soaked, ready for rum/wine).
- **Coffee** — Café Santo Domingo, Monte Alto.
- **Merengue and bachata CDs / vinyl** — Juan Luis Guerra classics.
- **Faceless dolls (Muñecas Limé)** — a symbolic representation of the Dominican people's mixed heritage.
- **Woven baskets and cigars** (small workshops in Higüey and Santiago).

### Where to shop
- **Mercado Modelo (Santo Domingo)** — traditional handicrafts.
- **Calle El Conde (Santo Domingo)** — pedestrian shopping street.
- **Blue Mall & Ágora Mall (Santo Domingo)** — modern shopping.
- **Sanctuary Cap Cana Village and Downtown Punta Cana** — high-end.
- **Ámbar Museum shops (Puerto Plata and Santo Domingo)** — reliable amber sourcing.
- **Larimar workshops (Bahoruco, near Barahona)** — direct-from-source.

### Bargaining
- **Markets and street stalls:** expect to bargain. Start at 50–60% of the asking price.
- **Reputable stores:** fixed prices.
- **Do so with a smile** — Dominicans enjoy the ritual.

### Duty-free at the airport
Rum, cigars, and coffee are cheapest either at the airport duty-free or at supermarkets — not at tourist shops in the resort area.

## What to bring home vs. leave

Prohibited or restricted:
- **Coral, turtle products, conch shells** — CITES-protected; do not buy.
- **Certain plants and seeds** — restricted at your home customs.
- **Amber and larimar** — legal; keep the receipt for customs.
- **Cigars** — check your home country's tobacco allowance.

## Cultural do's and don'ts (recap)

**Do:**
- Say hello when entering shops and taxis.
- Tip in cash and generously if service was good.
- Try to dance, even badly — it makes locals happy.
- Ask permission before photographing people.
- Learn a few Spanish phrases.
- Be patient with time — nothing runs on strict schedules outside airports.

**Don't:**
- Assume everyone speaks English outside tourist zones.
- Wear swimwear away from the beach.
- Discuss Haitian politics or migration unless prompted, and even then, listen more than opine.
- Photograph police, military, or airport-security areas.
- Buy coral or turtle products.
- Get into unlicensed taxis late at night.
- Litter — the country takes coastal cleanliness increasingly seriously.
