# Dominican Republic — Food and Drink

Dominican cuisine is a fusion of Taíno, Spanish, and African influences, with heavy use of rice, beans, root vegetables, pork, seafood, and plantains. It is unpretentious, generous, and reliably tasty. Beyond the resort buffet, there is a whole world of local food worth exploring.

---

## The national dish: La Bandera Dominicana

"The Dominican Flag" — the everyday lunch across the country:
- **White rice** (arroz blanco)
- **Red beans** (habichuelas rojas), stewed with garlic, onion, cilantro, and tomato
- **Stewed meat** — usually chicken (pollo guisado) or pork
- **Fried plantains** (tostones or maduros)
- **Small salad** — shredded cabbage, tomato, avocado

Served at every neighborhood restaurant ("comedor") for 250–400 DOP (US$4–7).

---

## Classic Dominican dishes to try

### Sancocho
A hearty seven-meat stew (chicken, beef, pork, goat, ham, sausage, longaniza) with yuca, plantain, and cilantro. National comfort food, especially for special occasions. **Sancocho de siete carnes** is the most elaborate version.

### Mangú
Mashed green plantains, served hot with sautéed red onion. The classic **Mangú** breakfast — "Los Tres Golpes" ("the three hits") — pairs it with fried salami, fried cheese, and fried egg.

### Chicharrón and Chicharrón de pollo
Deep-fried pork belly (chicharrón de cerdo) or crispy chicken chunks (chicharrón de pollo). Ubiquitous street food; excellent with lime and hot sauce.

### Pescado con coco
Fresh fish (typically snapper) simmered in coconut milk, garlic, tomato, and cilantro. A **Samaná specialty** — try it at any beachside restaurant along Playa Cosón or Las Terrenas.

### Moro de guandules
Rice cooked with pigeon peas, coconut, and cilantro. Traditionally served on Sundays and Christmas.

### La Bandera de mariscos (seafood platter)
Rice with shrimp, lobster, octopus, and calamari in a garlic-tomato sauce. A tourist restaurant staple.

### Pollo al carbón
Rotisserie-style spit-roasted chicken. Every gas station and roadside stand sells it. Under US$10 for a whole bird with rice, beans, and salad.

### Yaniqueques (Johnny cakes)
Deep-fried flatbread. Beach vendors sell them stuffed with cheese, chicken, or crab. A **Boca Chica** and **Juan Dolio** classic.

### Mofongo
Better known in Puerto Rico, but common in the DR: mashed fried plantain with garlic and pork cracklings, served as a mound with meat or seafood on top.

### Locrio
The Dominican version of paella — rice cooked in one pot with chicken, seafood, or sausage. Home-cooking classic.

### Pastelón de plátano
A "lasagna" of ripe plantain layers filled with ground beef and cheese. A very Dominican comfort dish.

## Street food and snacks

- **Empanadas de yuca** — cassava-dough turnovers with chicken or cheese.
- **Quipes** — Lebanese-influenced bulgur wheat croquettes stuffed with beef.
- **Pastelitos** — small fried pastry pockets with meat, cheese, or shrimp.
- **Fritura mixta** — a fried mix of pork, plantain, and yuca. Roadside classic.
- **Habichuelas con dulce** — sweet cream of red beans with milk, sugar, coconut, cinnamon, sweet potato, and raisins. Made especially at Easter.
- **Concón** — the crunchy layer of burnt rice at the bottom of the pot, prized as a delicacy.

---

## Fruits to try

- **Chinola** (passion fruit) — sold as juice everywhere.
- **Guanábana** (soursop) — creamy, tart tropical fruit; wonderful in smoothies.
- **Mamey** — dense red-orange flesh, tastes like sweet potato meets cantaloupe.
- **Zapote** — small brown fruit, custardy flesh.
- **Níspero** — small brown fruit with a caramel/malt flavor.
- **Cacao** — visit a cocoa farm in San Francisco de Macorís or Samaná; taste the raw pod.
- **Coco frío** — cold green coconuts sold on beaches for 100 DOP; drink the water, then eat the meat.

## Coffee

The DR grows excellent coffee in the Cordillera Central. Look for **Café Santo Domingo, Monte Alto, Induban, and Café Monte Real**. Coffee is typically served in small cups, very strong and sweet ("café con leche" is smaller than an American latte).

Farm tours in **Jarabacoa** and **Polo (Barahona)** let you follow the bean from picking to cup.

---

## Chocolate

Dominican cacao is world-class. Two brands to seek out:
- **Kah Kow** — bean-to-bar in the Zona Colonial. Excellent factory tour + tasting.
- **Chocolate Cortés** — supermarket-friendly.

Farm visits: **Ruta del Cacao** in San Francisco de Macorís, or ChocoMuseo in Zona Colonial.

## Drinks

### Rum
The country's national drink. Three iconic distilleries:
- **Brugal** — Puerto Plata; light and crisp; the flagship "Brugal Añejo" is the standard.
- **Barceló** — Santo Domingo; smoother; the "Imperial" is the widely-loved sipper.
- **Bermúdez** — San Pedro; the oldest (1852); often overlooked but excellent.

Also worth trying: **Ron Matusalem** (though technically Cuban-Dominican), **Vizcaya VXOP**, **Opthimus**.

### Mamajuana
A local herbal-rum-red-wine infusion, sometimes called "the Dominican Viagra." Sold in bottles pre-soaked with tree bark, herbs, and honey — you just top it up with rum and wine. Sweet, spicy, and mildly medicinal. Tourist favorite as souvenir.

### Presidente
The national beer. A crisp light pilsner, drunk cold ("bien fría"). Also **Bohemia** (a slightly maltier competitor) and **Presidente Light**.

### Cocktails
- **Piña colada** — invented in the Caribbean; still ubiquitous.
- **Cuba libre** — rum, coke, lime.
- **Santo Libre** — rum with lemon soda; the everyday Dominican drink.
- **Morir Soñando** — non-alcoholic "die dreaming" of orange juice, milk, sugar, and ice. Sounds bizarre; is glorious.

### Fresh juices
Every restaurant offers **jugos naturales**: passion fruit (chinola), tamarind, papaya, mango, cherry. Ask for "sin azúcar" if you want less sugar.

## Where to eat

### At the resort
Buffets are the workhorse. À la carte specialty restaurants (Italian, Japanese, French, Mexican) are usually included; **book on arrival day** as popular slots fill quickly for the week.

### Off-resort — recommended by region

**Punta Cana / Bávaro (outside the resort strip):**
- **Jellyfish** — beach dining, elevated Caribbean cuisine.
- **Balicana** — Balinese-Caribbean fusion.
- **Passion by Martín Berasategui** — Michelin-starred chef's outpost at Paradisus Palma Real.
- **La Yola** (Puntacana Resort) — over-the-water seafood.

**Santo Domingo:**
- **Adrian Tropical** — the local classic on the Malecón; Dominican comfort food.
- **Mesón D'Bari** — traditional Dominican in Zona Colonial.
- **Meson de la Cava** — inside a real cave.
- **El Conuco** — folk-themed, live music, tourist-friendly.
- **La Alpargatería** — upscale contemporary; try the sancocho.
- **Buche Perico** — modern Dominican street-food-elevated.

**Las Terrenas (Samaná):**
- **La Terrasse** — legendary French bistro on the main strip.
- **Barrio Latino** — the local expat gathering spot.
- **El Cabito** (Las Galeras) — cliff-edge sunset restaurant.
- **Le Café de París** — French bakery + café.

**Cabarete / Sosúa:**
- **Bliss** — beachfront Italian on Cabarete Beach.
- **La Casita de Papi** — locally famous seafood shack.

**Bayahibe:**
- **Mare Nuestro** — Italian seafood on the fishing-village beach.
- **Saona Café** — reliable local menu.

### Comedores and food trucks (budget)
Every town has family-run "comedores" serving La Bandera lunch for 250–400 DOP. Follow local workers around 12:30 for the busiest (= freshest) ones.

## Dietary notes

- **Vegetarian:** Do-able. Rice, beans, plantains, avocado, and cassava are cornerstones. Beans are sometimes cooked with a bit of pork — ask "sin cerdo, por favor."
- **Vegan:** Harder outside major cities and resorts. Bring supplements; expect to eat lots of rice and vegetables.
- **Gluten-free:** Easier — most Dominican staples (rice, cassava, plantain, corn) are naturally gluten-free.
- **Kosher / Halal:** Very limited; specialty resorts in Punta Cana can arrange with 48-hour notice.

---

## Food safety

- **Tap water:** Do not drink. Even locals drink bottled or filtered water. All resorts and reputable restaurants use filtered water and ice.
- **Fresh salads and unpeeled fruits from street vendors:** eat cautiously outside resorts.
- **Seafood:** avoid at cheap roadside stalls in inland regions.
- **Overall:** the DR has a lower rate of traveler's stomach issues than many tropical destinations; resort food is exceptionally well-controlled.
